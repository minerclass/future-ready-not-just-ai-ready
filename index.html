Future Ready,
Not Just AI Ready

What should stay difficult? A district leader's lens for deciding what AI should make easier.

Micah Miner, CETL · Director of Innovation and Technology, Beach Park CCSD 3 · Author, AI Goes to School

Before we start

Two questions.

Talk to the people at your table. I want honest answers, not impressive ones.

Question one

Name one thing that got easier in your district this year because of AI.

Question two

Now name one thing your students got better at because of AI.

The gap

Those are not the same question.

"Got easier"

  • Mostly about adults
  • Measured in workload
  • Visible this year
  • Easy to point to

"Got better"

  • About students
  • Measured in capability
  • Visible in independent work
  • Harder to infer from output alone

It is easy to answer the first and believe we have answered the second. The question underneath both is the one this hour is about: what should stay difficult?

The distinction

Procurement question

AI ready

Do we have the tools, the policy, the training, the data agreements? Necessary work. Answerable in a year.

Graduate question

Future ready

Can our students think, judge, and make themselves understood when the tool is not there? Answerable in a decade.

First, a measurement problem

Screen time is the wrong question.

Districts have argued about minutes. The next question is what those minutes were doing.

What the report counts

  • Minutes on the device
  • Apps opened, tabs visited
  • Assignments marked complete
  • Time on task, flagged green

What it cannot see

  • Whether the student did the thinking
  • Whether the words were theirs
  • Whether anything got harder, then got easier
  • Whether they could do it again on Monday

Why the metric fails

Two students. Forty-five minutes each.

Both students 45 min · complete · on task Your data cannot tell them apart.

The better question

Not how long the screen was on. What the screen made easy.

The reframe

Friction is not one thing.

I call this pedagogical friction: the effort learning requires. The work, slowness, and struggle that make growth possible.

Remove barriers in any layer. Preserve the thinking, dialogue, and responsibility required by the learning goal.

An Illinois high school junior, writing in the September 29 ISBE Weekly: “To learn is to endure a necessary friction.”
Reduce this layer

The base: the conditions

The conditions that make learning possible at all. Time, access, technology, policy, the building itself.

A student who cannot reach an archive. A family who cannot read the notice. A teacher who loses an evening to scheduling. These are barriers to learning, not the learning itself. Removing them expands who gets to do the work.

The three you protect

Head, Room, and World.

Click each one

Head · the thinking work

Forming an idea, holding two things that contradict each other, staying in not-knowing long enough to get somewhere.

When a tool does this: the answer still appears. The thinking moved from the student to the system.

Room · the work of being understood

Finding the words, ordering them, imagining a reader who does not already agree with you.

When a tool does this: writing is not the transcription of a finished thought. Writing is how the thought gets finished. Fluent prose a student did not build skips the part where understanding forms.

World · the work of becoming

Who the student turns into by staying with something difficult until it is done.

When a tool does this: a student who never finishes something difficult may never learn that they can. That is not information. It is formation.

Researchers call this unproductive success

Green dashboard, unfinished learning

Illustrative pattern, not measured student data.

The part that is nobody's fault

The instructional judgment stays with you.

Tools embody decisions about what to make easier. Districts still have to judge whether those decisions serve the learning goal and the learner's needs.

Which means the judgment call can only live with the adults. No procurement process, no setting, and no vendor assurance moves that responsibility off your desk.

The pattern across all four

The vendor decides what the tool makes easy. You decide what it means.

Two districts can run identical software and get opposite outcomes. Privacy, bias, equity, and academic integrity remain non-negotiable safeguards. For a district, digital citizenship and sound AI policy come down to the same decision: what students still do for themselves. Did anyone make that decision on purpose?
